\section{Introduction: The Flat-World Promise}
\label{sec:intro}

On 15 January 2022 the submarine volcano Hunga Tonga--Hunga Ha\okina apai
erupted with extraordinary force. The tsunami and ash were
the immediate disaster. The quieter one came \tongaCableCutOffshoreKm{}
kilometres off the coast of Nuku\okina alofa, where the eruption severed the
\tongaCableKm-kilometre fibre-optic cable that was the kingdom's only
digital link to the rest of the world \parencite{subtel2022tonga,euronews2022tonga}.
For \tongaOutageDays{} days, roughly \tongaOutageWeeks{} weeks, a country of
some \popTongaK{} thousand people was mostly offline: with only limited satellite links to carry calls, payments and
aid coordination \parencite{sbs2022tonga}. The nearest suitable repair ship
had to sail from Papua New Guinea \parencite{pina2022ship}.

The episode is a parable for an argument this paper makes. Three decades ago
it was widely expected that falling transport and communication costs would
make geography matter less: distance was dying \parencite{cairncross1997} and
the world was becoming flat \parencite{friedman2005}. In many ways it did.
Tonga has a fibre cable at all only because of that revolution; in
\windowEnd{} more of its people were online than ever. But integration also
changed the \emph{nature} of remoteness. When a society runs on just-in-time
shipping, real-time payments and cloud software, distance stops being a cost
paid once, at the border, and becomes a recurring tax paid on every shipment,
every byte, every specialist and every negotiation. And because those systems
are tied together, the taxes compound.

\begin{pullquote}
The equalisers of our age are also the multipliers of distance.
\end{pullquote}

This paper sets out that argument and, as far as public data allow, tests it.
It makes three claims. First, remoteness should be measured, not assumed, and
being \emph{far} must be separated from being \emph{small}: they are different
handicaps with different remedies (\cref{sec:edge}). Second, in a fast-moving,
integrated economy the costs of distance recur and reinforce one another
across trade, technology, health, people and climate
(\cref{sec:tax,sec:trade,sec:tech,sec:health,sec:people,sec:climate}).
Third, acceleration means that small lags in adopting and \emph{using} new
technology can widen into structural gaps (\cref{sec:accel}).

Each claim comes with a warning label. Iceland, Singapore, Mauritius and the
Faroe Islands are peripheral and prosperous, and \cref{sec:escape} asks how
they escaped. Most remote places are richer than they were; the argument here
is about relative position, volatility and control, not absolute decline.
Governance and colonial history shape outcomes at least as much as geography,
and a cross-section of countries cannot fully separate them. Several of the
charts below show gaps that are \emph{narrowing}, and they are reported as
such. Where the data contradicted a claim in early drafts, the claim was
revised.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Remoteness as a trajectory.} The question is not whether a place is
on the edge, but whether the edge is moving towards it or away from it---and
what policy can do to bend that path.
\end{keyidea}

The rest of the paper proceeds from definition (\cref{sec:edge}) to mechanism
(\cref{sec:tax}), through five arenas where the tax is paid, to the
acceleration argument (\cref{sec:accel}), the exceptions
(\cref{sec:escape}), the strongest objections (\cref{sec:counter}) and what
can be done (\cref{sec:paying}). Every number is generated by code from public
data; the appendix and the accompanying repository document how.
